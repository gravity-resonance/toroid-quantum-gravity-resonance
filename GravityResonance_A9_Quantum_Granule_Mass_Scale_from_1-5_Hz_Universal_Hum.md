## 5. The 1/*f* Spectrum and the Absence of a Low-Frequency Cutoff

The preceding analysis focused on the prominent 1–5 Hz band. However, in electronic systems this hum is part of a broader 1/*f* (flicker noise) spectrum that raises two profound questions: does the spectrum have a lower frequency cutoff going down, and does it have a natural upper boundary going up? The answers to both questions carry direct implications for the vacuum granule picture.

### The Upper Boundary: Conventional Noise, Not Physics

Above roughly 100–1000 Hz, conventional noise sources — Johnson thermal noise, shot noise, electromagnetic interference, and mechanical resonances — grow rapidly and completely dominate the measured spectrum. Any vacuum granule contribution at higher frequencies would simply be buried under this floor, invisible rather than absent.

### The Lower Boundary: Without a Confirmed Lower Limit

Going downward in frequency, no confirmed floor has been found. Voss and Clarke measured equilibrium resistance fluctuations in continuous metal films, demonstrating 1/*f* behavior with no low-frequency flattening [Voss1975, Voss1976]. Their analysis showed explicitly that the standard theoretical treatment of uncorrelated temperature fluctuations predicts a spectrum that should flatten at low frequencies — yet the measured spectrum continues as 1/*f* without flattening, a discrepancy they noted as unresolved [Voss1976]. Bid, Bora and Raychaudhuri (2005) measured 1/*f* noise in silver nanowires from 1 mHz to 10 Hz, noting that the lower frequency limit of 1 mHz was set by practical measurement constraints (temperature stability and drift), not by any observed physical floor [Bid2005]. Hu et al. (2021), analyzing 1/*f* noise in the FAST radio telescope receiver system, found a knee frequency reduced to 6×10⁻⁴ Hz after foreground subtraction, but honestly noted that an apparent low-frequency plateau in their data "may also be affected by the finite length of available data" rather than representing a true physical cutoff [HuFAST2021]. Caloyannides (1974), as reviewed by Milotti (2002), reported 1/*f* behavior in operational amplifiers down to ~10⁻⁶ Hz over months-long runs with no observed cutoff [Milotti2002].

Within all currently accessible measurement windows, no universal low-frequency floor has been established within the measured range. It must be noted honestly that below 10⁻⁴ Hz the evidence becomes technically very difficult: measurement runs of months to years are required, and separating genuine 1/*f* behavior from instrumental drift is increasingly challenging. The extrapolation to still lower frequencies should be treated as a theoretical possibility motivating future measurement, not an established fact.

Other windows may open as instruments reach lower frequencies and quieter noise floors.

In this sense the noise spectrum of a precision instrument acts as an **inverted telescope**: where a conventional telescope looks outward to larger and larger scales, a precision noise measurement looks inward, through quieter and quieter frequency windows, toward smaller and smaller mass scales in the quantum vacuum. Building quieter instruments that reach lower frequencies does not merely reduce noise — it opens a deeper window into the structure of the vacuum itself.

### Phase-Based Interaction with the Vacuum

These entities should not be interpreted as localized particles in the conventional sense. A mass in the range derived here implies Compton wavelengths extending from hundreds of meters to solar system scales and beyond. At such scales the appropriate picture is not that of discrete objects traveling through space, but of extended phase structures in the quantum vacuum.

In this view, the detector does not "collide" with a granule. Instead, the detector's quantum degrees of freedom — particularly conduction electrons — evolve within a background modulation produced by the granule's slowly varying wave structure. The interaction is therefore best understood as a spatial modulation of the electron's energy state as it traverses the granule's wave, rather than a resonance between two oscillators of matching frequency.

A natural objection arises: the electron's own Compton frequency is ~10²⁰ Hz — twenty orders of magnitude above the granule's 1–5 Hz. How can two oscillators so far apart in frequency interact?

The answer is that they do not need to match frequencies. The granule's wave acts as a slowly varying background modulation of the local vacuum — analogous to a long ocean swell carrying short surface ripples. A slow deep-water swell and the fast ripples riding on its surface have completely different frequencies. They do not resonate with each other. The swell simply modulates the medium; the ripples ride within that modulated medium, and any observer on the surface feels the slow rhythm of the swell regardless of the frequency of the ripples.

The same principle governs AM radio transmission. The carrier wave and the audio signal have completely different frequencies. The carrier wave modulates the medium; the receiver reads that slow modulation — not the carrier frequency itself — and recovers the signal. The granule is the swell, the vacuum is the medium, and the electron is the receiver reading the slow 1–5 Hz modulation imprinted on the vacuum by the granule's passage. No frequency matching is required.

This perspective echoes an older tradition in theoretical physics in which waves and geometry are treated as primary elements of physical reality. The wave–particle duality introduced by Louis de Broglie and the concept of spacetime structure explored by John Archibald Wheeler both suggest that matter and geometry may ultimately represent different aspects of a deeper underlying wave structure. The granule picture explored here follows that tradition by asking whether macroscopic background signals — such as the ubiquitous 1–5 Hz hum — might encode information about that structure.

## 7. Relation to Quantum Gravity: Three Independent Convergences

The mass range derived here has not been previously obtained from the empirical 1–5 Hz noise record. However, it converges independently with estimates from three separate branches of theoretical physics:

**Loop quantum gravity:** Rovelli and Smolin derived a minimum area at the Planck scale from the discrete spectrum of the area operator [Rovelli1990]. Although LQG predicts discreteness primarily near the Planck scale, the possibility of additional effective vacuum excitations at much lower energy scales is not excluded by the theory. The existence of discrete geometric degrees of freedom is conceptually consistent with the granule picture proposed here.

**Vacuum condensate theory:** Consoli (2015) derived a fermion condensate mass from vacuum structure arguments in an overlapping range [Consoli2015].

**Spacetime foam:** Wheeler (1955) and Hawking (1978) proposed granular vacuum structure at small scales [Wheeler1955, Hawking1978]. While neither fixed a specific grain mass, their frameworks are consistent with a hierarchy of vacuum structure extending to the scales derived here.

None of these works derived the mass scale from the observed 1–5 Hz hum. The convergence is therefore independent agreement across three theoretical routes and one empirical route — not circular reasoning. While striking, this does not constitute proof. It does, however, provide strong motivation for experimental investigation.

### Possible Relation to Dark Matter

An intriguing possibility is that the vacuum granule modes inferred here may relate to the broader problem of dark matter. Cosmological observations indicate that roughly 85% of the matter content of the universe is non-luminous and interacts only weakly with ordinary matter. Despite decades of experimental effort, no confirmed particle candidate has yet been detected locally.

The mass range derived in this work falls within the regime explored in certain ultralight dark-matter models, where scalar or vector fields possess extremely small masses and correspondingly large Compton wavelengths. No direct identification of the granules with any specific dark-matter particle is proposed here. However, their properties — extreme diffuseness, electromagnetic transparency, and phase-based interactions — share qualitative features with those expected of ultralight background fields. This similarity in scale suggests a possible conceptual connection worth further investigation, while remaining speculative pending experimental confirmation.

It is therefore conceivable that the 1–5 Hz vacuum signal discussed here represents a local manifestation of a broader cosmological field. Whether such excitations contribute to the cosmological dark-matter density remains an open question requiring further theoretical and observational study.

## 9. Limitations of the Present Model

The present analysis is intentionally heuristic and relies on order-of-magnitude estimates derived from simple quantum relations. The proposed mass scales should therefore be regarded as indicative rather than precise predictions.

This simplicity is not a weakness but a deliberate choice with historical precedent. Max Planck derived the characteristic scale of blackbody radiation through dimensional analysis of *h*, *c*, and *k*_B before anyone possessed the quantum mechanical framework to explain why it worked. Enrico Fermi estimated the energy yield of the first atomic bomb by dropping scraps of paper and watching them drift — a back-of-envelope calculation that proved correct to within a factor of two. In both cases, the simple path through dimensional reasoning identified the correct physical scale and pointed toward the deeper theory waiting to be built. The power of dimensional reasoning lies not in its complexity but in its ability to identify the correct scale from which deeper theory can grow.

Two principal uncertainties remain. First, environmental and instrumental noise sources cannot yet be fully excluded as contributors to the observed 1–5 Hz background. Second, the physical mechanism by which hypothetical vacuum granules would couple to macroscopic detectors such as gravimeters, seismometers, or interferometers remains unspecified. A full quantum field theoretic treatment of the granule–electron coupling is beyond the scope of the present work and is identified as a priority for future theoretical development.

Future experimental and theoretical work will be required to determine whether the observed frequency band reflects a genuine property of the quantum vacuum or arises from more conventional terrestrial processes. The present work should therefore be regarded as an exploratory framework intended to identify characteristic scales and suggest experimental tests, rather than a complete theoretical model of vacuum structure.
